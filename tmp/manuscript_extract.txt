                                       INTRODUCTION

1.1 Background of the Study

         Brain tumors present important challenges in diagnosis and treatment,
and gliomas are among the most common types of primary malignant brain
tumors. Assessing their extent requires careful examination of medical images,
particularly MRI scans. Recent advances in deep learning have made
automated image segmentation a promising way to support this assessment.
This section discusses gliomas and their assessment, the role of MRI, deep-
learning approaches to segmentation, and the proposed NeuroQuant system.

1.1.1 Gliomas and the Need for Tumor Assessment

         Gliomas are a major group of primary brain tumors. The International
Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) reports that more than 250,000 primary
malignant brain tumors are diagnosed worldwide each year, and that gliomas
account for approximately 77% of these cases. Gliomas can differ in appearance
and extent, making assessment of both the tumor and surrounding affected
tissue important.

1.1.2 MRI in Glioma Assessment

         Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) helps clinicians examine gliomas
using different image contrasts. T1, contrast-enhanced T1 (T1Gd or T1ce), T2,
and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences provide
complementary information about brain anatomy, contrast enhancement, and
abnormal tissue around a tumor. Reviewing these sequences together can help
delineate tumor subregions and estimate their extent.

         Manual delineation of these regions can be time-consuming and may
vary between readers. Research using the Brain Tumor Segmentation (BraTS)
benchmark addresses these challenges and evaluates automated segmentation
methods using multiparametric MRI data.

1.1.3 Deep Learning for Tumor Segmentation

         Deep-learning methods can assist with tumor segmentation. A three-
dimensional U-Net, for example, processes volumetric image data and
produces a spatial segmentation mask. When the mask is combined with the
image's voxel spacing, the volumes of its segmented regions can be estimated.

         These measurements may help clinicians review tumor extent and
compare imaging over time. They should be interpreted alongside the original
images and clinical context and should not replace professional judgment.

1.1.4 The Proposed NeuroQuant System

         This study proposes NeuroQuant, a decision-support system for glioma
segmentation and volumetric quantification using multiparametric MRI. The
current system loads T1, T1ce, T2, and FLAIR volumes, runs segmentation
locally using an ONNX model, displays the resulting mask in 2D and 3D views,
and calculates active tumor core, peritumoral edema, and total tumor volume
using voxel spacing.

         NeuroQuant is intended to support qualified clinicians in reviewing
imaging findings. Its automated segmentation and measurements are outputs
for professional review; the system does not establish a diagnosis or replace
clinical judgment.

1.1.5 Rationale for the Study

         The project addresses the time and variability involved in manually
delineating tumor regions by bringing MRI import, automated segmentation,
visualization, and volumetric measurement into one desktop workflow.
Running segmentation locally allows that analysis step to be performed
without requiring a network connection, provided the workstation and model
are available.

         Scope clarification: NeuroQuant also has cloud features for radiologist
scan uploads and sharing prepared preview images. Since your focus here is
local segmentation and quantification, mention those features only if they are
part of the study's stated scope.

1.3 Objectives of the Study

         To design, develop, test, and evaluate NeuroQuant, a desktop clinical
decision-support system that locally imports and displays multi-parametric
brain MRI studies, generates glioma segmentation overlays, calculates tumor
core and edema volumes, and prepares reports for clinician review.

1.4 Scope and Limitations of the Study

         This study covers the design, development, testing, and evaluation of
NeuroQuant, a desktop clinical decision-support system for reviewing brain
MRI studies and assisting with glioma segmentation and volume measurement.

         The system imports supported image files, including readable DICOM
series, NIfTI, NRRD, MetaImage, and Analyze files. For segmentation, the user
must confirm one volume for each required MRI sequence: T1, contrast-
enhanced T1 (T1ce/T1Gd), T2, and FLAIR. The system provides axial, coronal,
and sagittal views, displays model-generated segmentation overlays, calculates
active tumor core, peritumoral edema, and total tumor volumes using voxel
spacing, stores records locally, and can export a summary report.

         MRI import, segmentation, visualization, and local record management
are designed to work without internet access. Optional AI, collaboration, cloud
storage or synchronization, and account-recovery functions require an internet
connection when used.

         The study is limited to the functions and supported inputs defined in the
SRS. Segmentation requires all four specified MRI sequences to be readable and
correctly assigned. CT may be imported for viewing, but it is not used by the
MRI segmentation model and does not support CT-based tumor segmentation.

         NeuroQuant does not provide a clinical diagnosis, replace clinician
judgment, or integrate with a hospital PACS. It is designed for an individual
workstation and does not provide a shared client-server patient database. The

              REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE AND STUDIES

2.1 Introduction

         This chapter reviews literature and studies related to glioma MRI,
automated tumor segmentation, volumetric measurement, and medical image
analysis software. It summarizes relevant systems, compares their features with
the proposed NeuroQuant system, and identifies how the study's scope
responds to the needs documented in the requirements interviews. The chapter
concludes with the study's input-process-output conceptual framework.

2.2 Review of Related Literature and Studies

         This section reviews literature and studies on glioma MRI, tumor
segmentation, and volumetric measurement relevant to the proposed
NeuroQuant system.

2.2.1 BraTS Tumor Regions on Multimodal MRI

         Glioma MRI assessment uses multiple image sequences because
different contrasts show different aspects of a tumor and surrounding tissue.
The BraTS benchmark literature describes using multi-parametric MRI and
tumor sub-region labels to develop and compare automated glioma
segmentation methods. Its commonly discussed regions include the whole
tumor, tumor core, and enhancing tumor. NeuroQuant's SRS defines a project-
specific, simpler label set: background, active tumor core, and peritumoral
edema. These label sets should not be treated as identical. (Menze et al., 2015;
Baid et al., 2021)

2.2.2 U-Net Architecture for Image Segmentation

         Deep-learning segmentation methods can automate the delineation of
image regions. The U-Net architecture was developed for biomedical image
segmentation, while U-Net extends this approach by automatically configuring
parts of the segmentation pipeline, such as preprocessing and network settings,
for a task. These studies support the technical basis for exploring a 3D U-Net
model, but results reported by other teams do not establish NeuroQuant's
accuracy. (Ronneberger et al., 2015; Isensee et al., 2021)

2.2.3 Tumor Volume Measurement from MRI Masks

         Segmentation masks can also support volumetric measurement.
Research on automated brain-tumor segmentation has evaluated predicted
tumor volumes against manually delineated volumes, showing why
segmentation and measurement need to be assessed separately. For
NeuroQuant, volumes are calculated from predicted voxel labels and the MRI's
physical voxel spacing. The project's evaluation dataset, metrics, and
acceptance thresholds still need to be defined in the SRS. (Huang et al., 2021)

2.2.4 3D Dense U-Net Glioma Segmentation

         A study using 3D Dense U-Nets trained on multimodal MRI evaluated
whole-tumor, tumor-core, and enhancing-tumor segmentation across BraTS
and an independent clinical dataset. It supports studying 3D deep-learning
segmentation across multiple sources, while its BraTS region definitions do not
directly match NeuroQuant's labels. (Zhao et al., 2020)

2.2.5 3D FusionNet Multimodal Segmentation

         3D FusionNet study combined multiple MRI modalities and assessed
predicted tumor regions with overlap and boundary-distance measures. Its
results highlight the value of evaluating both how much predicted and
reference regions overlap and how far their boundaries differ. The study's
reported performance is specific to its dataset and label scheme. (Segmentation
of glioblastomas via 3D FusionNet)

2.2.6 Longitudinal Glioma Volume Assessment

         Research on post-treatment diffuse glioma used a 3D nnU-Net with T1,
T2, post-contrast T1, and FLAIR images to segment tissue regions and assess
longitudinal changes. This is relevant to multimodal segmentation and follow-
up measurement, though its post-treatment tissue labels differ from
NeuroQuant's project-specific classes. (Longitudinal Assessment of
Posttreatment Diffuse Glioma Tissue Volumes)

2.3 Synthesis of the Reviewed Literature and Studies

         The reviewed literature shows that glioma segmentation commonly uses
multiple MRI sequences because each sequence provides different information

about the tumor and surrounding tissue. BraTS provides a widely used
benchmark for developing and comparing segmentation methods, with
reference labels for regions such as whole tumor, tumor core, and enhancing
tumor. These benchmark labels provide useful context, but they are not
identical to NeuroQuant's simpler project-specific classes: background, active
tumor core, and peritumoral edema.

         Research on U-Net, 3D U-Net, nnU-Net, and related models supports
the technical feasibility of using deep learning to segment brain tumors in
multimodal MRI. Studies also show that segmentation performance can vary
by region and dataset. Smaller or less clearly defined tumor regions can be more
challenging to segment. Results from those studies provide methodological
context for NeuroQuant's proposed 3D U-Net; they do not establish the
accuracy or clinical performance of NeuroQuant's model.

         The literature also connects segmentation with volumetric assessment.
Predicted labels can be converted into physical volumes using voxel counts and
MRI voxel spacing, and published studies have used automated segmentation
to support tumor-burden measurement and longitudinal assessment. This
makes segmentation quality and measurement quality related but distinct
evaluation concerns: a volume calculation can be implemented correctly while
still reflecting errors in the predicted mask.

         Taken together, the reviewed work provides a basis for NeuroQuant's
approach: process multimodal MRI, predict the project's three labels, and
derive volumes from the resulting masks and image spacing. NeuroQuant will
need its own evaluation using a defined dataset, reference annotations mapped
to its label set, suitable segmentation and volume metrics, and acceptance
thresholds. These details remain to be specified in the SRS before claims about
NeuroQuant's performance can be made.
